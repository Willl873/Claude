**Buffett's summary verdict**: 🟡 **Mixed, trending toward concerning.** The earnings themselves are real and high-quality. But the cash-flow and financing picture is deteriorating in a specific, quantifiable way this quarter — capex is stepping up again (to ~$70B) and, for the first time, equity dilution is explicitly part of the funding plan.

# PHASE 3: Editorial Polish — Published Article Version

## Oracle Beat Estimates — So Why Is It Also Asking Shareholders for More Money?

Oracle just had a genuinely good quarter. Revenue crossed **$67 billion** for the first time ever. Cloud infrastructure revenue **nearly doubled** (+93%). Earnings beat estimates and management **raised** next year's profit guidance. On the surface, this is exactly the kind of print that should send a stock higher.

But buried in the same earnings call was a second story that matters just as much: Oracle also told investors it needs **$70 billion** for next year's data-center buildout — and for the first time, part of that money will come from **selling new shares**, not just borrowing.

**Here's the plain-English version of why that matters.**

### The Three Things You Actually Need to Know

**1. The business itself is genuinely doing well.** Think of Oracle as two companies stapled together: an old, boring, extremely profitable database business, and a brand-new AI data-center business that's growing at nearly 100% a year. Both did their jobs this quarter — the old business showed customers locking in more future subscriptions, and the new business kept expanding fast.

**2. But the backlog everyone's excited about pays out slowly.** Oracle has a jaw-dropping **$638 billion** in signed contracts (called "RPO"). This quarter, for the first time, management told us exactly when that money actually shows up: only **12%** converts to real revenue within a year. More than **half of it** — 54% — is more than three years away. Big number, long wait.

**3. Oracle just told shareholders: "we need more cash, and some of it comes from you."** Building AI data centers is eye-wateringly expensive. Next year alone, Oracle plans to spend about **$70 billion**. To pay for it, the company is raising **$40 billion** — half in new debt (fine, normal), and **half by selling $20 billion of new stock**. Selling new stock means every existing share represents a slightly smaller slice of the company. That's a cost to current shareholders that wasn't part of the story a few months ago.

### Why the Stock's Reaction Was Confusing

If you saw Oracle's stock do something weird on earnings day — up a little, then people arguing about whether it's actually bad news — here's why: **the earnings were good, but the financing news was a genuine surprise.** Markets were digesting two contradictory signals in the same 30 minutes: "we're growing incredibly fast" and "we need to sell you new stock to keep growing this fast." Both can be true at once, and reasonable investors can disagree about which one matters more.

### The One Number That Explains Everything Else

About half of Oracle's giant backlog comes from a single customer: **OpenAI**, in a $300 billion, five-year deal. OpenAI is not yet a profitable company. Every question about Oracle right now really boils down to one thing: **can OpenAI actually pay for what it signed up for, on the schedule Oracle is building capacity for?** This quarter, that question officially became part of the standard conversation on every Oracle earnings call — not just something skeptics on the internet were saying.

### So What Should You Do?

**If you already own Oracle**: hold what you have, but don't rush to buy more right now. Wait to see how the new $20 billion stock sale actually gets priced — that will tell you the real dilution cost.

**If you're thinking about buying**: this isn't a "back up the truck" quarter or a "run away" quarter — it's a "watch the next one closely" quarter. Specifically watch whether the $76.6 billion of backlog due within a year actually shows up as real, collected revenue.

**The one thing to track going forward**: not the total backlog number (it will probably keep growing and grabbing headlines) — watch **how fast the near-term slice of it turns into cash**, and whether Oracle's biggest customer's own finances stay healthy enough to pay the bill.
