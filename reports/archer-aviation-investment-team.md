## 🟢 business-analyst (Duan Yongping lens)

**One-line business model**: Archer is building an electric vertical-takeoff-and-landing (eVTOL) aircraft — "Midnight" — designed to fly short urban routes (e.g., airport-to-downtown) as a faster, quieter alternative to helicopters or ground transport, monetized eventually through direct operation and/or aircraft sales to partners.

**Where the business actually is today**: this is **not yet a revenue-generating airline or aircraft manufacturer in any meaningful sense** — Q1 2026 revenue was $1.6M against $171.7M of R&D spend alone. The entire current "business" is a pre-commercial regulatory and engineering program, not an operating company with a demonstrated customer relationship at scale.

**Real, concrete progress this cycle**: Archer became the **first eVTOL company to close Phase 3 of the FAA's four-phase Type Certification process** (April 2026), unlocking Type Inspection Authorization — a genuine, verifiable regulatory milestone, not just a promotional claim. The aircraft itself has real, specific characteristics: 4 passengers + pilot, 150 mph cruise, 1,000+ lb payload, ~45 dB noise level.

**Partnerships as evidence of "good business" characteristics**: United Airlines and Southwest Airlines partnerships, and — notably — **Stellantis has committed up to $400M** to help scale Archer's Georgia manufacturing facility to 650 aircraft/year by 2030. A major global automaker committing real manufacturing capital is a more meaningful signal of business credibility than a marketing partnership would be.

**Duan Yongping's test — is this a good business, and what's the "one sentence"?**
> "Archer isn't a business yet — it's a very well-funded, regulatorily-advanced bet on becoming one. The 'good business' question can't really be answered until Midnight is flying paying passengers at scale; right now, the honest answer is 'promising engineering and regulatory program,' not 'good business.'"

A pre-revenue company with a 2.5-year runway funding a capital-intensive manufacturing scale-up (the Stellantis-backed 650 aircraft/year target) will very likely need to raise more capital before or shortly after reaching meaningful revenue — the only real question is how dilutive that raise will be and at what share price.

**Valuation framing**: with a $3.7B+ market cap against $1.6M of quarterly revenue, no traditional multiple (P/E, P/S) is meaningful — this is priced entirely as an option on successful certification + commercial ramp, not as a going concern with cash flows to discount.
